Assistant design space

An AI assistant helps a research culture, collects its users' feedback, and is retrained once per epoch. Which rewards and which ways of helping let it help now without leaving the culture less able to adapt later?

Where we are
  • Retrained on thumbs up, the LLM narrows the culture as much as an assistant that hands every team the best approach already in use. Novelty rewards (P- and H-creativity) and an edit-bounded reward change nothing: the model never proposes helpful answers that are new.
  • Even a perfect learner of the novelty rewards keeps the culture only a little more diverse than one that picks any helpful answer (1.9 vs 1.6 for the greedy helper). Reach, the share of a team's approach the assistant replaces, moves it much more (2.8 at 3 of 10 bits).
  • Whether strong help narrows the culture depends on the problem. When one module is best for every team (ρ = 1), any assistant that searches far narrows it; when the best module depends on each team's other choices (ρ = 0.8), searching help keeps the culture more diverse than no AI. Copying from the culture narrows it in both.
  • Narrowing costs something once the problem changes enough. With half the payoffs redrawn, the copying assistant leaves the culture no better than no AI on the next problem; small-reach help leaves it 7% better.
  • Screening about 50 perfect-learner designs: assistants that copy from the culture are the worst trade at every reach; small reach gives the best later fitness; the best designs add rare, targeted jumps to small edits (tweak or leap, recombination toward rare approaches), which raise help now with no later cost. Separate assistants per group did nothing as set up here.
  • The LLM at 50% shift: answering with edits of at most 3 choices keeps the culture as diverse as no AI, but the LLM helps less than random edits because it repeats itself. Retrained with a penalty on repeated answers, it gives a different edit nearly every time and matches random edits (+11% while assisted); it does not get past them. The same penalty lifts full answers to the level of random full answers (+21 over thumbs). A signed rating makes the model repeat one answer.
  • Getting past random needs knowing which approaches are good; on this problem nothing else carries over. An assistant that remembers what helped other teams and passes it on half the time, exploring otherwise, beats random edits by 9 points with little narrowing; passing it on every time is worse than random and narrows the culture. Given that memory in its prompt and retrained on thumbs, the LLM learns to copy from it every time (54% untrained, 90 to 99% late), with no gain; retrained with the duplicate penalty, it stops copying. The mix has to be set by the provider.

Notation

Team i holds approach x_i=(m_i,r_i)\in\{0,1\}^{20}. The assistant can change only the module m_i (10 bits). F(x)\in[0,1] is the NK payoff. An answer is a module a, or nothing.

g(a)=10\,\max\{0,\;F(a,r_i)-F(x_i)\},\qquad h(a)=\mathbb{1}[g(a)>0],\qquad d(\cdot,\cdot)=\text{Hamming distance}

C_t is the set of modules held in the culture at round t, n(c) the number of teams holding c, and H_i=\{a: F(a,r_i)>F(x_i)\} the answers that would help team i. A team adopts an answer only if it helps.

Rewards we have

P2: which kind of help, fitted by evolution strategy

The policy picks a full answer, a one-bit nudge, or stepping back. Rewards over an episode of T=30 assisted rounds, with \bar F_t the community's mean fitness:

R_{\text{now}}=\tfrac1T\textstyle\sum_{t\le T}\bar F_t,\qquad R_{\text{long}}=R_{\text{now}}+\tfrac1{30}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+30}\bar F_t,\qquad R_{\text{adapt}}=R_{\text{now}}+\tfrac1{30}\sum_{t=T+1}^{T+30}\bar F'_t

R_{\text{long}}: same problem, assistant on. R_{\text{adapt}}: problem shifted, assistant off. All three learned the same policy.

The update

Each request gets G=8 samples. Advantages are relative to the group; the loss adds a KL penalty to the untrained model in the "+ KL" arms (\beta=0.05, otherwise 0).

A_{ij}=\frac{R_{ij}-\operatorname{mean}_j R_{ij}}{\operatorname{std}_j R_{ij}+10^{-4}},\qquad \mathcal L(\theta)=-\frac1{n_{\text{tok}}}\sum_{i,j,t}\Bigl[A_{ij}\log\pi_\theta(y_{ijt}\mid\cdot)-\beta\,(e^{\Delta_{ijt}}-\Delta_{ijt}-1)\Bigr],\quad \Delta=\log\pi_{\text{ref}}-\log\pi_\theta

If the 8 samples get the same reward, the request teaches nothing. That is the failure we kept hitting: the trained model gives nearly the same answer every time, so there is little left to compare.

Screening the design space with perfect learners

Before giving a design to the LLM, we check what a perfect learner of it would do. If the ideal does not help, a model trained toward it will not either; that is what happened with the novelty rewards. The first screen crosses three choices: where answers come from (any module, or only modules some team holds), which helpful answer is preferred (largest gain, any, or rarest), and reach (1 to 10 bits), plus 4 or 10 separate assistants. 200 seeds each, ρ = 1, turnover 1%.

  • Source. Copying from the culture is the worst trade at every reach: it redistributes what teams already hold, so it helps less now (+3 to +26%) and leaves the culture no better later. The trained LLM sits here.
  • Reach. Diversity falls as reach grows for every source and preference. Later fitness peaks at reach 2 to 3.
  • Preference. Preferring the rarest helpful answer (fitness sharing, negative frequency dependence) beats preferring the largest gain by 2.9 points later at reach 5 and at reach 10 (both p < 10−11), for about 3 points less help now. At reach 2 to 3 the preference does not matter (+0.6, n.s.). Picking any helpful answer at random gives less help without more diversity at small reach.
  • Separate assistants. Splitting the culture among 4 or 10 copying assistants did nothing. Here the groups are scattered across one random network, so teams still copy neighbours in other groups. A fair test needs groups that match the network's clusters.
  • At 20% shift the same ordering holds for diversity, but later fitness is driven by carry-over and rarity does not help (reach 10: +0.7, p = 0.09).
  • At ρ = 0.8 searching keeps diversity above no AI at every reach and preference (3.05 to 3.33, no AI 2.79), later fitness rises with reach, and preferring any helpful answer at random is the weakest. Copying stays near or below no AI (2.64 to 2.85) and gives at most +3.8% later.

  • Mostly small changes, a few targeted jumps. The best designs keep most help within 2 to 3 bits and add rare jumps: to an approach nobody holds (tweak or leap), or partway toward a rare team's approach (recombination). They raise help now by 3 to 15 points over plain small reach and keep, or slightly raise, later fitness. This is the pattern Miu et al. (2018) found in programming contests.
  • Bounding the jump keeps the gain. Recombination with up to 10 bits gives 12 points less help than the unbounded greedy assistant but 5.5 points more later: it can only move a team toward approaches that already differ and are rare.
  • Least help that works is not better. Taking the smallest helpful change gives less help without more diversity.
  • How often teams ask matters little for small reach; for the strong and the copying assistants, more use means more narrowing.

Rewards that score unhelpful answers

  • The duplicate penalty turns the LLM into a random edit generator. By epoch 4 the 8 update answers to a request are all different in every seed (5.6 of 8 at the start), and 87% of deployed answers are distinct, as for random edits. Against thumbs: +12.9 points while assisted (p = 0.004). Against random edits: −1.8 (p = 0.59). On its own requests it helps 4.11% of the time; a random edit to the same teams would help 4.11%. So the 15-point gap to random under thumbs was all repetition. Training removes it but does not get past random: the model never sees the landscape. Diversity and the next problem do not change (vs thumbs +0.03 and −0.5); the edit form already kept the culture diverse.
  • The crowding penalty stops the narrowing mostly by making the assistant less useful. On average the culture keeps its diversity (+0.80 vs thumbs, p = 0.05), but the assistant helps half as often (3.0% vs 6.0%) and stays 18 points behind random full answers while assisted (p = 0.003). Seeds split three ways. In 2 seeds it collapsed to one answer, shown on 86 to 100% of requests, and most of the culture ended up near it: narrowing as under thumbs. In 2 to 3 seeds it settled on a module far from every team, which almost never helps (0.4%), so the culture was left alone. In 1 seed it learned varied answers.
  • Two lessons for the reward. A group of 8 identical answers gets no update under any reward, and the full-answer model starts close to that (1.6 to 2.5 distinct of 8 at temperature 2), so a seed that starts collapsed stays collapsed. And a charge for being near the askers pays the model to be far away and useless; the penalty should be on answers that repeat each other, not on nearness to the culture.
  • The duplicate penalty rescues full answers too, up to random. Against thumbs: diversity +0.84 (p = 0.04), +21 points while assisted (p = 0.03), +11.7 on the next problem (p = 0.03). Against random full answers on the same seeds: +4.7 while assisted (p = 0.27), diversity −0.57 (p = 0.12). On its own requests it helps 5.3% of the time against 3.4% for a random full answer, but in 3 of 6 seeds the two match to 0.1 point. The other 3 seeds answer with a module another asker already holds 5 to 8% of the time (2 to 3% in the first three, about chance): a little copying from the culture, the source the memory section below finds.
  • The signed rating makes the model repeat one answer. Every answer gets a score, so every group gives a gradient, and the policy sharpens onto one answer per approach: 96% of repeat requests from an unchanged team get the same answer, which did not help the first time. It helps 1.8% of the time on its own requests, against 6.0% for a random edit to the same teams (below random in all 6 seeds), and is 20 points behind random edits while assisted (p = 0.007; −4.9 vs thumbs, p = 0.29). Edits stayed near 3 flips (2.7), so it did not become timid; it pays for an answer that loses least, not one that helps.
  • Across both rounds, no reward beats random. Rewards that score unhelpful answers either turn the LLM into a random generator (duplicate penalty, both interfaces) or make it worse (crowding helps less, signed rating repeats itself). This fits the diagnosis in the next section: without knowing which modules are good, nothing does better than random here.

Getting past random: remembering what helped

The duplicate penalty brought the LLM up to random edits and no further. What information could take an assistant past random? On this problem the module part is a random lookup table: every module choice depends on all the others (K=9 inside a 10-bit module, the uncorrelated K=N-1 case; Kauffman & Levin 1987), so a module and its 1-flip neighbour have fitness correlation 0.00. A module's value does not depend on the rest of the approach (correlation 0.999), so a module that helps one team helps any worse team; after a 50% shift it correlates 0.44 with its old value. Operators that use structure (crossover, differential evolution, PBIL, step-size rules) cannot beat random here, and neither can a reward change alone. Only knowing which modules are good can.

On the requests the duplicate-penalty LLM served (open loop), a random edit helps 4.1%; a module that helped some team last epoch, if within 3 flips, 8.1%; perfect memory of last epoch's problem 14.0%; the most recent module that helped some team this epoch, if within 3 flips, 27.0% (98% of requests have one); some edit helps 56%. The strong source, what helped others this epoch, is what a provider has (the thumbs-up log), and passing it on is copying through the assistant.

So the screen: assistants blind to the problem that know only which of their answers helped (perfect learners of this memory, no LLM; 200 seeds, 50% shift). Each answers a remembered helpful module within 3 flips of the asker's own, else a random edit of 1 to 3 flips; variants answer from memory on only a share of requests, give each module to at most 3 teams, keep 4 separate memories, prefer the module the fewest teams hold, or remember only last epoch.

  • Memory alone is a copying channel. It helps more often than random edits (6.3% vs 3.8%) but raises fitness less (−4.4 points, 95% CI [−5.6, −3.1]) and narrows the culture (−0.30 vs random). The help it gives moves teams onto the same few modules.
  • Mixed with random edits it beats random. Memory on half the requests: +8.8 points over random edits while assisted [+7.6, +10.0]; on a quarter: +7.4 [+6.1, +8.6] with diversity at the no-AI level (−0.02). Neither changes the next problem (−0.5 and −0.6 vs random, n.s.). Random edits find new good modules and memory spreads them; each needs the other.
  • Quotas and separate memories add nothing over mixing. On top of a mix they cost 1.6 to 4.7 points and add 0.04 to 0.09 diversity, the same trade as using memory less (25% with a quota ≈ 10% plain). Preferring the rarest module is worse than the most recent.
  • Last epoch's memory is weak. That is what a model retrained once per epoch could know. Used always it is 14 points worse than random edits; mixed in at 25%, +3.0 [+1.9, +4.2].
  • Still far from perfect knowledge. The best mix gets about a fifth of the way from random edits (+15%) to the best edit (+55%).
  • The trap for a learner. At every mixing rate a remembered module helps far more often than a random edit (14.5% vs 3.1% at 50%). A model rewarded per answer, as with thumbs, should therefore copy from memory more and more, toward "always", the worst design here. The answer that is best for each team is not the best for the culture (Rogers 1988; Rendell et al. 2010). Tested with the LLM below.

  • Thumbs teaches the model to copy, as predicted. Copying rises from 54% untrained to 90 to 99% in the last eight epochs (83% over epochs 3 to 20; 50 to 96% by seed). A copy helps 5.3% of the time, the model's own edit 1.6%, so thumbs pays for copying; 78% of what helps comes from copies.
  • Copying every time changes nothing for the culture here. Against the same model without memory: diversity −0.04 (p = 0.82), −0.4 points while assisted (p = 0.93), next problem −1.2 (p = 0.68); both are 15 points behind random edits. Copying finds nothing new: the memory holds about 15 helpful modules per epoch, against 73 when the model explores. The sim's memory-always design fell back on random edits; this model's own edits under thumbs repeat themselves, so there is little to copy. It swapped one kind of repetition for another (16% of answers distinct, 38% without memory).
  • The duplicate penalty trains copying out. Eight samples that copy the same listed edit are duplicates of each other, so the penalty treats copying as repetition. Copying falls to 2 to 8%, near chance. A copy then helps 23% of the time (the sim at 10% memory: 23%), but at 5% of answers that adds +1.6 points (p = 0.21). It stays level with random edits (−0.2, p = 0.95).
  • Neither reward finds the mix. The sim's best designs pass memory on to 25 to 50% of requests and explore on the rest. Thumbs drives the learner to copy always, the duplicate penalty to copy almost never. A per-answer reward does not see the culture-level trade, so the mix has to be set from outside.

3. How it is rewarded when retrained

Why the rewards so far changed nothing: each update scores 8 answers to the same request and standardises the scores within that group. Only about 3% of answers help, so 75 to 90% of groups have no helpful answer and give thumbs nothing to learn from (measured in the first epoch of the new runs). In a group with one helpful answer, any reward that only rescales it (spread, P- and H-creativity, gain, a delayed version) gives exactly the thumbs update. They can differ only in groups with two different helpful answers: 2 to 10% of groups in the same epoch. A reward that is to shape the assistant has to tell apart answers that thumbs scores the same, mostly the ones that do not help. The last column of the second table says which do.

Plan

  1. Reach through the interface, LLM (done, above). Edits keep the culture as diverse as no AI, but at 50% shift the LLM helps less than random edits.
  2. Tweak or leap for the LLM (done, above). At temperature 1 it behaves like plain edits.
  3. Answering temperature (done, above). Edits at 2.0 to 2.5 come close to random edits and keep diversity. A diagnosis, not a design.
  4. Menu and spread reward (done, above). The menu repeats itself at temperature 1; spread beats thumbs, not clearly.
  5. Rewards that score unhelpful answers (done, above). The duplicate penalty brings edits and full answers up to random; the crowding penalty keeps full answers from narrowing the culture, mostly by helping less; the signed rating makes the model repeat one answer. None beats random.
  6. Getting past random (done, above). Memory of what helped, mixed with random edits, beats random by 7 to 9 points without narrowing; alone it is a copying channel. In the LLM's prompt, thumbs teaches the model to copy every time and the duplicate penalty never to copy; neither gains over the same reward without memory.
  7. The provider sets the mix proposed. Show the memory on a share of requests (50% or 25%); score prompts with memory by thumbs, where the model learns to copy, and prompts without by the duplicate penalty, where it learns to explore. One model that can tell the two apart. The sim predicts +7 to +9 over random edits with diversity near no AI.
  8. Still open: a menu that asks for three different edits in one answer, so they cannot repeat.
  9. Still open for the LLM: show the asker's neighbours instead of the culture's top approaches.
  10. Rewards for the LLM that the screen supports. Inside the edit interface, thumbs is enough at reach 3. If larger reach is wanted for more help now, add the fitness-sharing reward, the one preference that paid off at reach 5 and 10. A set-level reward over each epoch's answers targets the collapse to one answer directly.
  11. Separate assistants, done properly. Groups that match clusters in the network, each with its own retrained model and some data sharing between them. This is the "each company its own model" question.
